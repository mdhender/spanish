Every Spanish infinitive ends in `-ar`, `-er`, or `-ir`. That ending is not decoration: it
sorts the verb into one of three classes, and the class determines which endings it takes
in every tense. Learn one verb from each class and you can conjugate most of the language.

This page is about why that works, and where it stops working. The paradigms themselves live
on the model verbs' reference pages: [hablar](/reference/hablar/),
[correr](/reference/correr/), and [sufrir](/reference/sufrir/).

## Why there are three

Latin had four conjugation classes. Two of them sounded alike and collapsed together on the
way to Spanish, leaving three. Nothing about the meaning of a verb decides its class — it is
inherited, and the infinitive ending is simply the label that survived.

`-ar` is the one open class. When Spanish takes in a new verb it becomes an `-ar` verb
almost without exception: *googlear*, *tuitear*, *chatear*. The other two classes are
effectively closed. So `-ar` is both the largest class and the one you can guess into
safely; `-er` and `-ir` you have to know.

## What a model verb is for

A regular verb is a stem plus an ending. Take the infinitive, drop the last two letters, and
what remains is the stem — `habl-`, `corr-`, `sufr-`. Everything after that is supplied by a
table that depends only on the class, the tense, and the person.

That is what makes a model verb useful. The endings on [hablar](/reference/hablar/) are not
facts about *hablar*; they are facts about the `-ar` class. Learn them once and *caminar*,
*trabajar*, *estudiar*, and several thousand others come with them.

| Class | Model | Stem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `-ar` | [hablar](/reference/hablar/) | habl- |
| `-er` | [correr](/reference/correr/) | corr- |
| `-ir` | [sufrir](/reference/sufrir/) | sufr- |

## The three classes are less different than they look

Set the three paradigms side by side and the `-er` and `-ir` classes turn out to be nearly
the same class wearing different labels.

Across the presente simple, pretérito indefinido, and pretérito imperfecto — eighteen
person-and-tense slots — `-er` and `-ir` verbs take **identical** endings in sixteen. They
part company in exactly two, both in the presente simple:

| | nosotros | vosotros |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `-er` | corr**emos** | corr**éis** |
| `-ir` | sufr**imos** | sufr**ís** |

Everywhere else in those tenses, if you know the `-er` form you know the `-ir` form. The
pretérito indefinido and pretérito imperfecto do not distinguish them at all — *corrí* and
*sufrí*, *corría* and *sufría*, take the same endings throughout.

`-ar`, by contrast, really is its own thing, and it is the class that stands apart in almost
every tense.

## The future ignores the classes entirely

The futuro simple is the odd one out, and in a useful way: it is not built on the stem at
all. It is built on the **whole infinitive**, with one set of endings shared by all three
classes.

*hablar* + é → hablaré  ·  *correr* + é → correré  ·  *sufrir* + é → sufriré

The endings are `-é, -ás, -á, -emos, -éis, -án`, and they are the same whatever the verb
class. So the futuro simple is the one tense where the three-way split does not exist —
there is nothing to choose between, and no class to remember.

## One quirk worth knowing

For `-ar` and `-ir` verbs, the nosotros form is the same in the presente simple and the
pretérito indefinido. *Hablamos* is both "we speak" and "we spoke"; *sufrimos* is both "we
suffer" and "we suffered". Only context tells them apart.

`-er` verbs are the exception: *corremos* is present, *corrimos* is past. Which means the
class that is otherwise nearly identical to `-ir` is the one that keeps this distinction.

## Where models stop helping

The catch is that the verbs you need most are the least likely to be regular.

Frequency and irregularity travel together in Spanish, as in most languages: the more a verb
is used, the more it resists levelling. The result is that the handful of verbs you reach for
constantly are precisely the ones no model covers — [ser](/reference/ser/),
[estar](/reference/estar/), [ir](/reference/ir/), [ver](/reference/ver/),
[poder](/reference/poder/). Each has to be learned on its own terms.

Even [andar](/reference/andar/), which looks like a plain `-ar` verb and behaves like one
almost everywhere, breaks in the pretérito indefinido: *anduve*, not *andé*.

So the three models are not a complete system. They are a very good bet on the long tail —
the thousands of ordinary verbs you will meet once each — while the short list of verbs you
will meet a hundred times a day has to be memorised. That is a reasonable division of labour,
and it is worth knowing which side of it a new verb falls on before assuming a pattern.
