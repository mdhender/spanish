title: Ser and estar

English has one verb where Spanish has two. *I am tired* and *I am a teacher* use the same
word; *estoy cansado* and *soy profesor* do not. Learners usually meet a rule for choosing
between them within the first week, find it works, and then find it stops working. This
page is about why the rule breaks and what is actually going on underneath.

For the forms themselves, see the reference pages for [ser](/reference/ser/) and
[estar](/reference/estar/).

## They were never the same verb

The split is not a quirk Spanish invented. It inherited two separate Latin verbs and kept
both.

*Ser* descends from Latin *esse*, the verb of being and existence — the ancestor of English
*is* and *essence*. A second Latin verb, *sedēre* "to sit", got tangled up with it along the
way and supplied some of its forms, which is part of why *ser* is so irregular.

*Estar* descends from Latin *stāre*, "to stand" — the same root that gives English *stand*,
*status*, *statue*, and *state*.

That etymology is the whole idea in miniature. One verb is about what a thing **is**. The
other is about how a thing **stands** — the position or condition you find it in. Spanish
never merged them because it never needed to; English is the odd one out for having
flattened the distinction.

## Why "permanent vs. temporary" fails

The first rule most learners are given is that *ser* is for permanent qualities and *estar*
for temporary ones. It is a useful lie: it gets a beginner through many real sentences
before it collapses. It collapses on cases like these.

*Está muerto* — "he is dead." As permanent as a condition gets, and it takes *estar*.

*Es joven* — "she is young." Youth is the most temporary thing there is, and it takes *ser*.

Permanence is not the axis. Death and youth sort the wrong way round because the question
was never how long something lasts.

## Classifying vs. situating

A better frame: *ser* **classifies**, *estar* **situates**.

*Ser* puts the subject into a category. *Soy profesor* files me under teachers. *Es
mexicana* files her under Mexicans. *Mi hermana es alta* says tallness is one of the
properties by which you would identify my sister. None of these claim to be eternal — I can
change careers, and my sister may have been short at eight — but each answers *what is it?*

*Estar* reports the condition the subject is in. *Estoy cansado*, *el café está frío*, *la
puerta está cerrada*. These answer *how is it, right now?* The subject is not being sorted
into a class; it is being located in a state.

Now the two counterexamples behave. *Está muerto* takes *estar* because death is a state you
end up in — the condition someone is in, not the category they belong to. *Es joven* takes
*ser* because youth is being used to classify: it is what she is, in the way tall or
Mexican would be.

## The baseline you are measuring against

There is a second idea worth having, because it explains the sentences that feel most alien.

*Estar* often carries a sense of departure from an expected norm. It does not just report a
state; it implies the state is a change from some baseline.

This is why *Es guapa* and *¡Qué guapa estás!* are both fine and mean different things. The
first classifies: she is a good-looking person. The second says she looks especially good
right now — a departure from her own ordinary. The compliment lives entirely in the choice
of verb.

It also explains why *estar* shows up in places permanence would forbid it. If a state is
being framed as the outcome of something, as a change from before, *estar* is available no
matter how long it lasts.

## Adjectives that change meaning outright

With some adjectives the choice does not shade the meaning, it swaps it. These are worth
knowing because a wrong verb here does not sound slightly off — it says something else.

| Adjective | With *ser* | With *estar* |
| --- | --- | --- |
| aburrido | boring | bored |
| listo | clever | ready |
| rico | rich | tasty |
| verde | green (in colour) | unripe |
| malo | bad, wicked | ill |
| vivo | sharp, lively | alive |

The pattern is the one above, not a list of exceptions. *Ser aburrido* classifies you as a
boring person; *estar aburrido* reports the state you are in. *La banana es verde* describes
what colour the banana is; *la banana está verde* says it is not ready to eat.

## The apparent exception: location

Location looks like it breaks the system. *Estar* handles where things are — *Juan está en
la fiesta*, *Madrid está en España*. But events take *ser*: *La fiesta es en mi casa*, "the
party is at my house."

This is not an exception, it is the distinction doing exactly what it should. A party is not
an object sitting somewhere; it is an event, and where an event happens is part of what the
event *is*. Say *la fiesta está en mi casa* and you have described the party as a thing
currently located in your living room, like a sofa.

## With participles: action vs. result

The same split runs through the passive. *Ser* plus a participle describes the action;
*estar* plus a participle describes the state it left behind.

*La puerta fue cerrada por el portero* — the doorman closed the door. It reports an event.

*La puerta está cerrada* — the door is closed. It reports how the door is now, and says
nothing about who did it.

## What to do with this

The practical upshot is not another rule to apply. It is to stop translating *to be* at all.

English trained you to reach for one word and then wonder which Spanish verb it maps to.
That question has no reliable answer, because the mapping is one-to-two and the seam runs
somewhere English does not mark. The habit worth building instead is to ask what the
sentence is doing — sorting something into a category, or reporting the condition it is in —
and let that pick the verb.

The boundary is not perfectly sharp, and it moves. Speakers differ on cases like *soy
casado* versus *estoy casado* for "I am married," and the choice varies by region as much as
by logic. That is normal for a distinction this old. The core holds even where the edges
blur.
